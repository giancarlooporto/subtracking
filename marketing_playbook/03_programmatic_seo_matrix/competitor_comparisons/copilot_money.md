## 2. Competitive Context & Psychological Trigger

Copilot Money has earned high acclaim for its aesthetic, neon-infused Apple-native design. It won an Apple Design Award and captured design-conscious consumers who were tired of outdated financial software like Quicken or Mint.

However, Copilot introduces **three severe friction points**:
1. **The $95/Year "Aesthetic Tax"**: Copilot charges **$95 per year (or $13 per month / $156 per year)**. For users who primarily want to keep track of their recurring subscriptions, software licenses, and streaming services, paying $95 annually is unjustifiable.
2. **Strict Apple Walled Garden Lock-In**: Copilot has historically built exclusively for macOS and iOS. It offers no web application for general browsers, no Windows desktop app, and no Android support. If you use a Windows PC at work or an Android phone in your pocket, Copilot is inaccessible.
3. **Mandatory Third-Party Bank Logins**: Despite its premium price tag, Copilot relies on third-party aggregators (Plaid and MX) to scrape your financial accounts, exposing users to the typical disconnection errors and security risks of bank credential aggregation.

SubTracking solves this by delivering a **gorgeous, modern, dark-mode subscription tracker** that runs on **any web browser (Web, Android, iOS, Windows, Mac)**, operates with **zero bank logins**, provides **10-year Ghost Cost wealth projections**, and costs **$0 for your local vault** or just **$8.99/year for Pro encrypted cloud sync**—saving users over $86 every single year.

### H2: The Copilot Dilemma: Gorgeous Design vs. Practical Reality

#### H3: The $95/Year Subscription Trap
There is profound irony in spending nearly $100 every year on an app whose primary promise is helping you reduce wasteful spending. At $95/year, Copilot itself is an expensive luxury subscription. Over 5 years, Copilot will extract **$475** from your bank account; over 10 years, that climbs to **$950** in nominal software fees. #### H3: Walled Gardens in a Cross-Platform World
Millions of consumers do not live exclusively in an Apple bubble. You might carry an iPhone for personal use while typing on a Dell or ThinkPad for work. Or you might prefer a Samsung Galaxy phone while editing video on a Mac Studio. Copilot's refusal to build a universal browser-accessible web app abandons cross-platform users.

#### H3: Feature Bloat vs. Focused Utility
Copilot attempts to be an all-in-one net worth tracker, investment monitor, and budgeting tool. In doing so, it introduces clutter: complex investment categorization, manual trade reconciliations, and messy transaction feeds. Many users do not want to manage their entire net worth in an app—they just want a dedicated, razor-sharp tool to stop recurring subscriptions from draining their bank accounts.
